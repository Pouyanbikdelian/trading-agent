The Trading Agent

A staged, defensive, self-auditing trading system for Interactive Brokers — one mechanical strategy that trades, and a committee of reasoning agents that argues about it and is scored on being right.

Two loops, one rule

A mechanical momentum strategy places every order through a hard-blocking risk manager. Eight LLM personas debate, predict and allocate a separate simulated book. The agents cannot reach the broker — that isolation is structural, and a test enforces it.

Everything is graded

Every agent view is a falsifiable prediction with a horizon, scored against real prices weeks later. Voices are weighted by track record, not by how confidently they were phrased.

Limits bind in code

Position, cluster, gross and drawdown caps are enforced by the risk manager, not requested in a prompt. Live trading requires two independent flags set deliberately.

It audits itself

A watchdog asks not whether jobs ran but what they produced — because the most expensive failures in this system have all been things that ran successfully and achieved nothing.

Chapter one

How the system works

Two independent loops sharing one read-only view of the world.

┌──────────────── APScheduler ────────────────┐ │ 26 jobs, one async process │ └───────┬─────────────────────────┬───────────┘ │ │ TRADING LOOP AGENT LOOP (mechanical · moves money) (reasoning · cannot move money) │ │ parquet price cache build_context() ↓ ↓ strategy → target weights 8 specialists → takes + predictions ↓ ↓ selection / combination challenger attacks the two loudest ↓ ↓ ═══ RISK MANAGER ═══ manager synthesises a posture (hard block) ↓ ↓ weekly PM allocates a SIMULATED book broker adapter → IBKR ↓ ↓ historian distils lessons (Fridays) paper account (CHF) ↓ memory: journal · predictions · trust

The trading loop

Once a day at 21:05 UTC the cycle loads prices, asks the configured strategy for target weights, and hands them to the risk manager. The risk manager is the only path to an order. It rejects, scales or accepts, and returns a decision with a reason. Nothing bypasses it — not a strategy, not an agent, not an operator mandate typed into Telegram at midnight.

Why weights and not orders

Strategies emit target weights. They never construct an order. That single interface choice is what makes the risk manager unbypassable: there is no other object a strategy could produce that the broker would accept.

The agent loop

Twice a week a committee of eight personas each receives a slice of a shared context blob — the quant sees ranked candidates and the macro dial, the creative agent is deliberately denied the book so it cannot anchor on what is already held. Each returns a stance plus a falsifiable prediction: subject, direction, horizon, confidence.

A challenger then attacks the two highest-conviction takes. A manager synthesises a posture from the takes, the objections, the deterministic guard flags, the lesson book and each agent's graded track record.

The design premise. Every prediction is stored and graded weeks later against real closes. That produces a hit rate and a Brier score per agent, and those numbers — not eloquence — decide how much a voice is worth. An agent wrong all month is a fade.

The portfolio manager

Weekly, the PM reads the week's debates, each agent's calibration, the sentinel's alert state, its own performance versus SPY, and a ranked candidate ladder. It returns target weights for a simulated sleeve.

Those weights then pass through a chain of caps that cannot be argued with: whitelist → 10% per single stock → maximum three ETF positions → 50% per correlated cluster → 100% gross. Anything off-list or operator-pinned is cut to cash, never substituted.

A hard-won lesson. The PM's charter once listed example tickers — "own JPM instead of XLF, LMT instead of ITA". With no ranked candidate feed in its prompt, those examples became the portfolio. It held the same names for a month at perfectly healthy turnover. Exemplars in a prompt are not illustrations; they are instructions.

Memory, and how the desk learns

Append-only by design. Nothing is deleted; beliefs are superseded or retired, and every one of them is supposed to be earned.

The counterfactual ledger

Most desks record what they did. This one also records what it declined — the ranked candidates it passed over — and measures their forward returns against the same benchmark over the same window. Without it, in a rising market, every name you didn't buy looks like a missed opportunity. It is the only comparison that establishes whether selection has edge.

The lesson lifecycle

The historian reads a week of evidence and may propose at most two lessons. A separate independent reviewer — a different charter, blind to authorship — votes on whether the week supported or contradicted existing claims. Promotion to established requires +3 net supporting evidence, so a lesson survives roughly a month of scrutiny before any agent treats it as true.

Every lesson must state its own boundaries:

applies_whenConditions where it holds — specific enough to tell whether today qualifies
fails_whenWhere it breaks, or where evidence is silent
invalidated_ifThe single observation that retires it
sampleDistinct names and period supporting it
Why the conditions are mandatory. A desk study tested "quality mega-caps rebound after extended drawdowns". Pooled over eight years it showed +5.8% excess versus SPY and a 74% hit rate — compelling, and it would have been recorded as established. Broken out by year, the hit rate has been below 50% every year since 2023. True on average, useless now. An unconditioned regularity is usually a description of the sample it was found in.

The operator is part of the loop

Instructions typed into Telegram are graded by tone — strong, medium, soft — deterministically, so the reading is predictable and is echoed back before it is acted on. A firm lesson enters the book established; anything softer arrives as a candidate the desk may disagree with. No mandate, however firm, can lift a risk cap.

Chapter four

Risk, safety and the live gates

The part of the system designed on the assumption that everything else will eventually be wrong.

Two flags, both deliberate

TRADING_ENV=live
ALLOW_LIVE_TRADING=true

Both are required; either alone does nothing. Settings.is_live_armed() enforces the conjunction and trading live refuses to start otherwise. This is the single most important invariant in the repository.

What the risk manager enforces

LimitDefaultBehaviour
Per-position10% of equityOrder scaled or rejected
Gross exposure100%No leverage
Short sellingDisabledNegative targets clamped to zero, always
Margin borrowing0%Cash-account behaviour; auto-loan blocked pre-submit
Daily loss2%One-day kill switch — reduce only
Drawdown15% from high-waterFull halt until a human resets
Configuration trap worth knowing. The risk manager reads its limits from .env only. The portfolio and position numbers in config/risk.yaml are not loaded by it — that file feeds the backtester and documents intent. Editing the YAML changes nothing about live risk.

Layered defences

Pre-trade

Whitelist, caps, cluster concentration, liquidity, currency-cash checks — all before an order exists.

Kill switches

Daily-loss and drawdown halts persist to disk, so a crash mid-day cannot forget that the switch was thrown.

Human gate

Cycle approval can require you to accept every rebalance by hand from Telegram before anything is submitted.

Position guards

ATR trailing stops and a profit ratchet, flowing through the audited command pipeline — never a new order path.

Sentinel

Intraday tripwires every 15 minutes. Information only: it alerts, it never convenes or trades.

Operator pins

/hold freezes a position out of the rebalance entirely, and the PM is hard-blocked from allocating to it.

The promotion path a strategy must survive

Backtest → walk-forward out-of-sample → paper for at least 30 days → live, sized down. No exceptions, including for ideas that feel obviously right. Especially those.

The watchdog philosophy

Freshness checks ask whether a file was touched. They cannot ask whether the work inside succeeded — and in this system that gap once hid three separate loops that had been running and achieving nothing for weeks, while every check stayed green.

So the watchdog now asks different questions. Did the committee produce a ruling? Did the grader score anything? Are predictions piling up past their due date? Has the historian ever run at all? Plus a catch-all that surfaces any exception logged in the last hour, grouped by call site.

The operating principle, stated plainly: a system that reports what it attempted rather than what it achieved will eventually lie to you, and it will do so politely, for months.

Chapter six

Design principles

Most of these were bought rather than chosen. Each one is here because something went wrong first.

1 · Limits belong in code, not in prompts

A rule stated in a charter is a suggestion. The PM's charter demanded "maximum three ETF positions" for months while a six-ETF book satisfied every cap that was actually enforced. If a constraint matters, it must be unbypassable.

2 · Never accept "it ran" as evidence

The scorecard, the counterfactual ledger and the weekly historian each ran on schedule and produced nothing, for weeks, silently. Count what was produced and alert when a counter that should move does not.

3 · Turnover is not a staleness metric

A book can churn weight every single week and hold the same names for a quarter. What matters is whether the name set moved.

4 · An unconditioned rule is a description of its sample

Every belief must state where it applies, where it fails, and what would retire it. A rule with no failure mode has not been thought through and will be believed for far too long.

5 · When two portfolios appear in one prompt, the key name is the whole disambiguation

A bare key called positions next to one called holdings caused an allocator to spend its entire review obligation on a portfolio it did not manage.

6 · Degrade, but say so

A broad exception handler around a decision path is correct — a bad symbol must not kill a cycle. It is only correct if the degradation is visible.

7 · Test the wiring, not just the parts

Every component that failed had passing unit tests. The bugs were in the seams nobody could reach. If a loop cannot be tested without standing up the whole system, extract it until it can.

8 · Generated data does not belong in a config directory

Machine-written files placed among human-curated ones inherit the wrong permissions and quietly stop being maintained.

Deliberate constraints

Long-onlyShorting is disabled at the risk manager, not by convention
No leverageGross capped at 100%; margin borrowing at 0%
No intradaySwing and position horizons only — days to months
Free data firstPaid sources require an explicit decision
Agents never tradeEnforced by an import test, not by good intentions

Chapter seven

Where it stands

An honest assessment, written to be read before risking money rather than after.

Solid

  • Risk gating, order state machine and kill switches — well covered and defensive by construction.
  • Protocol seams with real alternate implementations, so the simulator is not a mock.
  • Dependency direction enforced by a test, not by convention.
  • 982 tests, fast and hermetic; network and broker tests explicitly marked.
  • A mechanical strategy with a walk-forward-validated configuration and a live paper curve.

Unproven

  • The agent layer has no track record yet. Every mechanism that would have produced one was disconnected until 2026-08-06. Calibration starts from zero, honestly, from today.
  • The simulated PM is ahead of SPY over a short window that includes a month of a known bug. Encouraging; not evidence.
  • The lesson book predates working outcome measurement and should be read as unevidenced.

The road to live

  1. Deploy, then confirm calibration becomes non-empty within days.
  2. Thirty days of paper with the learning loops verifiably alive.
  3. Fund the live account with only what can be lost — the cleanest cap there is.
  4. Cycle approval on: accept every rebalance by hand for the first month.
  5. Work the first-month checklist weekly, and write down every incident.
The standard to hold. Success in month one is not profit — over one month on a small account, profit is noise, and reading it as a verdict is the most likely way to fool yourself. Success is that every trade is explainable, no invariant is violated, the graded-prediction count rises every week, and at least one real bug is found. A clean month with an empty incidents log usually means the monitoring is broken.
